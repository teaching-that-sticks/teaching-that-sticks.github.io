In 1990 George Miller drew a simple pyramid that changed how medical education thinks about assessment. At the bottom, a learner **knows** something. Above that, they **know how** to use it. Above that, they can **show how** in a controlled setting. At the top, they actually **do** it, in real practice, when it counts.

His point was that most assessment sits at the bottom. A multiple-choice test tells you what someone knows. It tells you very little about what they will do on a busy shift.

The pyramid also works in reverse, as a design tool. The verb in your learning outcome decides which level you're aiming at, and so what evidence you'll need. "Be aware of" can only ever be checked at the bottom: you can't watch someone being aware. "Demonstrate on a simulated patient" sits at *shows how*, and you can see it. "Uses the escalation tool on the ward" sits at *does*, and you'd need to observe it in practice.

## What this means for teaching

- **Choose your level on purpose.** Not every session needs to reach *does*. But if the change you want is in practice, an outcome at *knows* won't get you there.
- **Let the verb pick the evidence.** *Knows*: a quick quiz. *Knows how*: talk me through it. *Shows how*: a simulation or a supervised demonstration. *Does*: observation, case discussion or feedback from the workplace.
- **Higher levels need practice, not more content.** You can't get to *shows how* by adding slides. Learners need to rehearse the performance.
- **No single check proves *does*.** Real performance varies. Several short observations over time beat one big sign-off.

## Caveats

The pyramid is a model, not a measurement scale, and the levels blur in practice. Later writers added a level above *does* for professional identity, *is* (Cruess et al., 2016). And assessing the top of the pyramid well is hard: it takes repeated observation in real work rather than one test (van der Vleuten &amp; Schuwirth, 2005).
